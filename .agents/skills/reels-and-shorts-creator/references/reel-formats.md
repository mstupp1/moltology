# Reel formats

Video formats for daily reels. Each one is a shape viewers already recognise from their
feed, so they know within a second what kind of joke is coming. Pick one per reel through
the pitch-three step in the [writing playbook](../../../../content/writing-playbook.md).

Every format here fits the house constraints: the spoken hook is the first sentence and
the on-screen claim, about 50 to 65 words, six scenes plus the outro, one useful action
held back as the payoff, and one website invitation at the end. The sample scripts show
the direction. Never reuse one word for word, and never let a sample become a template:
change the situation, the jokes and the payoff every time.

How the six scenes usually split: scene 1 is the hook action, scenes 2 to 4 build the bit,
scene 5 is the turn or payoff, and scene 6 lands the useful action with the lobster. Bend
that when the format needs it.

Formats marked **recurring** can run as a named weekly segment (the same title card and
opening line, a new situation each time). That replaces the retired episodic series skill.

## Nature documentary (recurring: "Field notes")

- **Skeleton:** narrator observes "the human" in its habitat, describes one habit with total
  seriousness, the lobster shows the evolved behaviour, narrator notes the result.
- **Why it works:** Silas's deadpan voice at full strength; the joke is the framing, so
  the tip can be completely ordinary.
- **Trap:** turning it into a lore lecture. The human behaviour must be painfully familiar.

> Here we observe the human, moving one task to tomorrow. For the ninth day. The task is
> called "work on report." It has no edges. Nothing to grab. So the human does what its
> species does best. It checks email. The lobster has no such problem. It renames the
> task: "open the draft, type one heading." Done in four minutes. More field notes at
> moltology.org.

## POV

- **Skeleton:** "POV:" plus a moment the viewer has lived, a fast escalation with
  timestamps or counts, the reveal of how far it went, the lobster's one-move fix.
- **Why it works:** the viewer is the main character; timestamps give a built-in countdown.
- **Trap:** a generic POV ("POV: you're tired"). It needs a time, a number or a quote.

> POV: you opened your laptop to send one email. That was 9:04. It's now 11:30. You've
> answered six threads, renamed a folder and read about otters. The email is still a
> draft. The lobster's version: send the email before anything else opens. Then the
> otters. More small fixes at moltology.org.

## Roast list

- **Skeleton:** name the habit, list what it has "survived" or "done" with escalating
  specifics, then the one change that finally ends it.
- **Why it works:** the repetition builds rhythm and the escalation pulls to the end.
  Light roasting of the habit, never the person.
- **Trap:** roasting the viewer's character instead of the habit.

> "Work on report" has been on your list since September. It survived three Mondays. It
> survived a new notebook. It survived you colour-coding the list instead of doing the
> list. It will outlive us all, unless it gets a first step. "Open the draft. Type one
> heading." That one's gone by lunch. Try the audit at moltology.org.

## Fake infomercial

- **Skeleton:** "Are you tired of…", an over-the-top product reveal that is actually a tiny
  habit, deadpan feature list, "side effects", price (free).
- **Why it works:** everyone knows the ad rhythm, so the absurd product lands instantly.
- **Trap:** a real product pitch. The "product" is the habit, not a Moltology feature.

> Are you tired of meetings that could have been a sentence? Introducing: the sentence.
> Type what you'd say in the meeting. Hit send. That's it. No invite. No "can everyone
> see my screen." Results may include an empty Tuesday afternoon. Side effects: finishing
> things. The sentence is free. Learn the rest at moltology.org.

## Expectation vs reality

- **Skeleton:** the plan stated proudly, then reality minute by minute, then the absurd
  final state, then the lobster's fix applied before the plan starts.
- **Why it works:** the gap is the joke; minute markers give pacing for the cuts.
- **Trap:** making reality merely sad. The final state should be funny and specific.

> Expectation: a two-hour focus block. Reality: minute one, you check one thing. Minute
> four, a "quick call." Minute nine, you're sorting your desktop icons by colour. The
> block is gone and the icons look incredible. The lobster's trick: put the phone in
> another room before the timer starts, not after. Try the audit at moltology.org.

## Work forecast (recurring)

- **Skeleton:** weather-report delivery of the week's work conditions, a "front" moving
  in, visibility, and the forecaster's recommended shelter (the useful action).
- **Why it works:** a familiar broadcast rhythm that carries any topic and suits seasonal
  slots (storm season, holiday "travel delays").
- **Trap:** too many puns. One forecast joke per line.

> Here's your work forecast. This morning: heavy notification showers, with a 90 percent
> chance of "circling back." By noon, a meeting front moves in from accounting.
> Visibility: one task. Tonight: scattered guilt about the task. Forecasters recommend shelter: notifications off, door closed, one
> task until lunch. Stay dry out there. More forecasts at moltology.org.

## Reverse tutorial

- **Skeleton:** "How to [waste time on something]", numbered steps of the bad habit played
  as instructions, the step where it becomes awkward, then the real one-line method.
- **Why it works:** numbered steps hold attention; viewers recognise every step.
- **Trap:** too many steps for the word budget. Four or five, the last one the funniest.

> How to spend four hours not sending one email. Step one: open the email. Step two:
> decide it needs a better first line. Step three: check whether they replied to the last
> one. Step four: lunch. Step five: it's 4pm and now it's awkward. Or write two sentences
> and send it. Sent beats perfect. Learn how at moltology.org.

## Unpopular opinion

- **Skeleton:** the take, the evidence piled up with specifics, the counter-move, a short
  plain closing line.
- **Why it works:** mild disagreement makes people watch to see if they agree. Keep the
  take defensible and kind.
- **Trap:** a take nobody disagrees with ("rest is good").

> Unpopular opinion: your morning routine is too long. Cold plunge, journaling, lemon
> water, a podcast about morning routines. By 10am you've done eleven things and none of
> them were the work. The lobster's routine has one step: start the hardest task before
> checking anything. Not glamorous. Finished by lunch. Try the audit at moltology.org.

## Rating your excuses

- **Skeleton:** quote three excuses, give each a score out of ten with a one-line reason,
  the honest one scores highest, then the fix for that one.
- **Why it works:** scores create a reason to wait for the next line; the twist is the
  high score.
- **Trap:** scoring that shames. The narrator is amused, not disappointed.

> Rating your excuses for not starting. "I need a clear afternoon." Two out of ten. You've
> never had one. "I'll do it after this email." One out of ten. There is always another
> email. "I don't know where to start." Ten out of ten, honest. The fix: pick the smallest
> visible step and do only that. Learn how at moltology.org.

## Countdown

- **Skeleton:** "Three signs…", counted down so the best sign lands last, then one action.
- **Why it works:** the count is an open loop viewers wait out.
- **Trap:** three signs of equal weight. Escalate.

> Three signs your calendar is in charge. Three: you check it to find out what you think.
> Two: your focus time has moved more often than the meetings. One: you accepted an invite
> called "sync" without knowing what it syncs. Take one hour back this week. Block it, name
> it after the task, and decline over it. Try the audit at moltology.org.

## Ask a lobster (recurring)

- **Skeleton:** a mock street interview. Question to the lobster about a human habit, its
  calm verdict, its practical advice, a closing line that undercuts it.
- **Why it works:** a character with an opinion; the mascot gets a voice through quoted
  lines while Silas narrates.
- **Trap:** the lobster lecturing. It should be blunt, brief and a little smug.

> We asked a lobster about your inbox. "Two thousand unread? That's not an inbox. That's
> an archive with anxiety." What would it do? "Archive everything older than a week. If it
> mattered, they'll write again." Has it tried this? "I'm a lobster. I don't have email.
> That's why I'm calm." Learn how at moltology.org.

## Day in the life: human vs lobster

- **Skeleton:** alternating timestamps, human first, lobster second, the gap growing, then
  the one decision that made the difference.
- **Why it works:** a split structure that gives the edit a natural rhythm.
- **Trap:** too many timestamps for 60 words. Three is enough.

> A day in the life: you versus a lobster. 7am: you check your phone in bed. The lobster
> has no phone. 9am: you open 31 tabs. The lobster picks one rock. Noon: you're behind. The
> lobster is done and napping under the rock. The difference was one choice before
> breakfast: today's one thing. More field notes at moltology.org.

## Adding a format

Add one when a shape keeps showing up in the feed and none of the above covers it. Give it
the same four parts: skeleton, why it works on video, the trap, and an original sample
under 65 words that passes the playbook's boredom check.
